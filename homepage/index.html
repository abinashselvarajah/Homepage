Computervision

Synthetic vs. Real

Industrial recognition systems need lots of training images, and real ones are hard to come by. So we wanted to know: how far can you get with synthetic images instead?

We rebuilt a real dataset of Makeblock parts as 3D models in Unity and used the Perception tool to generate synthetic versions, playing with colour, rotation, texture, background and lighting to fill the gaps the real photos left. Then we trained classification and detection networks on different mixes of real and synthetic images and compared the results.

The short answer: synthetic data is a great addition to real data, as long as you're careful about how you randomise it.

Robotics

Autonomous Vehicles and Artificial Intelligence 2023

The challenge: get a TurtleBot3 around a racetrack using nothing but a camera and LiDAR, finding its own way between coloured cones.

A YOLOv5 model spots the cones, and we combined those detections with LiDAR distances to work out where each cone actually is. The robot's odometry kept drifting, so we used Cartographer SLAM to keep its position honest and collected the cones in an occupancy map. From there, the controller simply aims for the point halfway between the nearest blue and yellow cone.

Autonomous Vehicles and Artificial Intelligence 2025

Two years later, same task, much faster hardware: we brought the idea over to an F1TENTH racecar.

This time a RealSense camera and a YOLOv11 model find the cones. We trained the model on real images plus varying amounts of synthetic ones. The detections get combined with LiDAR data, clustered with DBSCAN and put on a map. The car still aims for the gap between the nearest blue and yellow cone, and when it can only see one colour, it guesses where the missing cone should be.

Human-Centered Robotics

In a sheltered workshop, a worker with cerebral palsy presses small rubber and metal parts together. Placing the metal part and pulling the lever are the toughest steps, so we built a robot assistant to help them.

We started by visiting the workshop, then wrote up a persona and requirements and worked through it in Scrum sprints. We programmed the ABB YuMi in Python, teaching it motion targets by hand. To grab loose parts, OpenCV finds each one and its orientation in a webcam image, and a 3D-printed holder keeps the metal part steady. The worker talks to the robot: “Hey YuMi” wakes it up, the OpenAI API handles a bit of small talk, and an emergency stop is always there in case something goes wrong.

Natural Language Processing

Membership Inference Attacks

Was this text part of a language model’s training data? That question matters a lot for copyright and data leaks, and membership inference attacks try to answer it. The catch is that a single attack isn't very reliable once the data starts to vary.

So instead of betting on one attack, I combine lots of them. I extended an existing framework from 10 to 644 attack features and swapped its linear combiner for stronger classifiers like XGBoost and an MLP. I tested it on Pythia-2.8B and 6.9B across eight subsets of The Pile.

Blue Team vs. Red Team

Differential privacy is supposed to keep training data private. But how well does it actually hold up when someone tries membership inference on it?

We played both sides. On defence, we trained a DistilBERT sentiment classifier on IMDB reviews with DP-SGD at different privacy budgets. On offence, we went after it with the RMIA and LiRA attacks and looked at how attack success, accuracy and F1 shift as the budget changes.

LLMs for Repurposing

Repurposing gives old products a second life, but coming up with a new use for them is tricky. Could LLMs help? I co-authored a paper (DESIGN 2026) that put GPT-5, Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro to the test.

We went through the literature and put together a validated dataset of 72 real repurposing cases. Every model got the same zero-shot prompts, asking for ten repurposing ideas and the properties each one would need. I helped set up these experiments, 3,120 runs in total. We then compared the answers with the documented cases and checked them by hand.

Spec Extraction by Distillation

Large language models are good at reading the spec pages in product manuals, but running them over thousands of manuals gets expensive fast, both in compute and in money. Can a small model learn to do the same job?

gpt-5.6-terra acts as the teacher: it turns manual pages into JSON grouped by physical component, or marks them as empty if there are no specs. We use those labels to fine-tune two small vision-language models, Qwen3-VL 2B and 4B, with LoRA. The training mix is 80 % spec pages and 20 % empty pages, so the students also learn what to skip. Finally, we check on unseen manuals whether they return valid JSON, spot empty pages and pick out the right components and fields.

Reinforcement Learning

Exploration vs. Exploitation

Every reinforcement learning agent has to decide between trying something new and sticking with what already works. We wanted to find out which exploration strategy does best in a maze.

We pitted constant and annealed ε-greedy, Count Based exploration and UCB against each other on a DQN agent. For the test bed we wrote our own Gymnasium maze generator, with 10×10 and 20×20 mazes, several seeds, and both dense and sparse rewards. Every run was tracked in Weights & Biases.

Graph Database

MaLeNa

If you want to reuse parts from old devices, you first need to know which devices share the same parts. For MaLeNa, I built that foundation: a graph database of phone specs.

A scraper pulls the datasheets from phonedb.net and stores each phone in Neo4j as a graph of its components and their properties. A final merge step folds identical components into a single shared node, so one Cypher query is enough to see which phones use the same display or battery.
